Six stages, one tiny transformer, trained in front of you

Every station below trains a real model with real gradients on your GPU, small enough to converge in under a minute. Beside each toy is the matching call in Marin, Stanford's open pipeline (JAX and Levanter). The toy uses characters instead of a 128k-token BPE vocabulary and two layers of width 48 instead of 32 layers of width 4096; the loss functions, the schedules, and the failure modes are the same.

Decoder vs encoder, the one picture

causal mask: token t sees ≤ t
full mask: every token sees all

That triangle is the whole difference between a GPT and a BERT at the architecture level. Everything else (the residual stream, attention heads, the MLP) is shared. The decoder is trained to predict the next token and can generate; the encoder is trained to fill blanks and produces a representation of the whole input, which is what an embedding model returns.

Marin, the real thing

Marin defines a run as a graph of steps, executed in dependency order like a Makefile: download → tokenize → train → evaluate. The beginner tutorial trains llama_nano on TinyStories with a hundred steps on CPU.

from experiments.llama import llama_nano
from levanter.optim.config import AdamConfig

    optimizer=AdamConfig(learning_rate=6e-4,
                         weight_decay=0.1),
    batch_size=32, seq_len=model.max_seq_len,
    num_train_steps=100,

Station 1

Data: text becomes windows of token ids

A model never sees text. It sees integers. Tokenization maps characters (here) or byte-pair pieces (in Marin) to ids, and training slices the id stream into fixed windows so a batch is a rectangle the GPU can chew.

Corpus

characters… vocab… windows… batch16 windows

One window, as the model sees it

Input is the window; the target is the same window shifted one to the left. Every position is a training example at once, which is why decoders learn so fast per token.

In Marin

from marin.processing.tokenize.tokenize import TokenizedCache

Marin tokenizes once into a cache and every later step depends on that cache, so re-running an experiment never re-tokenizes. The vocabulary is byte-pair pieces, which is why "the" is one id there and three ids here.

Station 2

Pretrain a decoder: minimize next-token cross-entropy

Two layers, width 48, three heads, causal attention. The loss is the average negative log-probability of the true next character. Watch it fall from ln(vocab) (uniform guessing) toward the corpus's own entropy, and watch the attention map organize itself.

Same loss. Marin's model is a Llama-shaped decoder (rotary positions, SwiGLU MLP); this toy uses learned positions and GELU so the code stays short. AdamW with weight decay 0.1 and a warmup-then-cosine schedule is the standard recipe; the toy uses Adam with a constant rate.

Station 3

Mid-training: change the data mix and cool the learning rate

Real runs don't end when pretraining ends. In the last stretch the mix shifts toward higher-quality or targeted data and the learning rate decays to near zero. Here: continue the station-2 model on a mix of stories and arithmetic while the rate cools, and watch the two domain losses trade off.

Marin expresses a mixture as weights per tokenized dataset; the loader samples proportionally. The cooldown is the tail of the learning-rate schedule. Two things to watch for, both visible in the toy: catastrophic forgetting of domain A when the mix is lopsided, and the sharp loss drop that a cooldown gives for free, which is why people report "cooldown" runs separately.

Try mix B at 0.9 with a constant rate: domain A loss climbs. Then 0.6 with cooldown: both settle.

Station 4

Post-training: SFT teaches the format, DPO teaches the preference

A pretrained model completes text. Post-training turns it into something that answers. Supervised fine-tuning trains only on the answer tokens of question/answer pairs. Direct preference optimization then pushes chosen answers above rejected ones relative to a frozen copy of the SFT model.

What each loss looks at

Highlighted tokens are the only ones SFT is graded on. The question is context, not target; grading it would just teach the model to parrot prompts.

L_DPO = −log σ( β [ log π(y_w|x) − log π_ref(y_w|x) − log π(y_l|x) + log π_ref(y_l|x) ] )

π is the policy being trained, π_ref the frozen SFT model, y_w the chosen answer, y_l the rejected one. The bracket is the implicit reward margin. β sets how far the policy may drift from the reference; too large and it collapses onto the preference data.

In Marin

SFT in Marin is another train_lm-style run over chat-formatted data with loss masking on the assistant turns, initialized from the pretrained checkpoint. Preference training runs on top of Levanter's DPO trainer with a frozen reference. The 8B and 32B retrospectives in the repo's experiment reports describe the exact mixes; the shape is what you see here: format first, then preference.

Marin's experiments live under experiments/; the post-training configs are per-run scripts rather than a single module, so this station quotes the shape, not a path.

Station 5

Encoder: masked language modeling, then contrastive pairs, then embeddings

Same transformer, mask removed. Stage one hides 15% of characters and asks the model to fill them (that's BERT, and it's how nomic-bert-2048 starts). Stage two throws away the fill-in head and trains the pooled vector so windows from the same document sit close and others sit far (InfoNCE). The result is an embedding model: text in, one vector out.

Before contrastive training the three topics overlap: MLM makes the model good at local prediction, not at global similarity. After a few hundred InfoNCE steps the clouds separate, and "nn purity" (how often a sentence's nearest neighbour shares its topic) climbs.

In the real recipe (nomic-embed, and Marin's encoder work)

L = −log exp(s(q,d⁺)/τ) / Σ_d exp(s(q,d)/τ)

Pretrain a BERT-shaped encoder with MLM (30% masking, 2048 tokens), then contrastive-train on ~235M weak pairs with batch 16k so each batch carries thousands of negatives, then fine-tune on curated pairs with mined hard negatives. Mean-pool, normalize, dot product. The toy does the same two stages with batch 32.

Station 6

Cluster the embeddings with k-means

Clustering needs no labels and no training; it's the first thing you do with a new embedding model to see whether the space means anything. Each step assigns points to the nearest centroid, then moves centroids to their members' mean. Purity compares the clusters to the hidden topics.

iteration 0inertia –purity –moved –

Points are the station-5 embeddings (PCA to 2-D for drawing; the assignment runs in the full 48-D space). Rings are centroids. Colour is the cluster; the small inner dot is the true topic, so a mismatch shows as two colours on one point.

Where this shows up in a pipeline

Data curation. Marin-style pipelines embed a crawl, cluster it, and use the clusters to balance the mixture, deduplicate near-copies, or drop junk regions of the space before tokenization. Clustering also grades an embedding model without labels: if k-means recovers your known topics, the encoder learned something usable.

inertia = Σ_i ‖x_i − μ_{c(i)}‖², purity = (1/N) Σ_k max_j |C_k ∩ T_j|

Try k=5 on three topics: two topics split, inertia drops, purity barely moves. That's the elbow argument in one click.

Station 7

Train a policy to paint with code

The loop from Hugging Face's "Train to paint with code": the model writes a program, the program is rendered, the picture is scored, and the policy is updated with GRPO. Nothing here is a language model (the policy is a table of logits over a tiny drawing language), but every part of the loop is the real one: sample a group, render each candidate, compute a composite reward with a gate, normalize advantages within the group, take a policy-gradient step with a KL leash to the starting policy.

The reward, and where the numbers come from

Each candidate is a program of 6 strokes; each stroke is a shape (disc or square), a position on an 8×8 grid, one of 4 sizes and 4 shades. The reward is a weighted sum in the same spirit as the post: a gate (0.05, the canvas is not blank), a coverage term (0.05), and similarity to the reference (0.90, a Dice-style overlap: one minus the squared error divided by the two energies, so flooding the canvas is punished as much as leaving it blank). The post uses a VLM pairwise judge and an aesthetic model in place of the similarity term; the structure is identical.

A_i = (r_i − mean(r)) / (std(r) + ε), ∇ = (1/G) Σ_i A_i ∇ log π(a_i) − β ∇ KL(π ‖ π₀)

Watch the blank rate first: in the post every run "first learned to stop producing bad paintings", and here the gate does the same job within a few steps. Then watch the mean climb past the best-ever line stalling: the policy sharpens (KL rises) around one good program, which is the exploration collapse that the group normalization delays but does not prevent.

Set β to 0 and G to 4 to see collapse early; set G to 16 to see how much of GRPO's stability is just a better baseline. Lab 15 has the full derivation and the 5090 recipe (recipes/paint_grpo.py) with a real language-model policy.

Station 8

Speculative decoding: draft cheaply, verify exactly

Decoding is one token per forward pass, and each pass is bound by reading the weights, not by arithmetic. So let a cheap draft propose k tokens and have the real model score all k in a single pass. Each proposal is accepted with probability min(1, p/q); at the first rejection you sample from the leftover max(0, p − q) and stop. The output has exactly the model's distribution, and you get more than one token per pass whenever the draft is any good.

verify passes 0tokens 0accept rate α –tokens / pass –theory (1−α^(k+1))/(1−α) –
(pretrain first, then generate; accepted draft characters are shown in brackets per pass)

Green-ish runs of accepted characters come from the draft; the character after a bracket is the model's correction. Switch to the unigram draft and watch α fall and tokens per pass approach 1: speculation only pays when the draft agrees with the model.

Why it is exact

accept x ~ q with prob min(1, p(x)/q(x)); else x ~ norm(max(0, p − q))

Summing the two branches gives min(p, q) + (1 − Σ min(p, q)) · max(0, p − q)/Σ max(0, p − q) = p, for every token. The draft only changes how many tokens you get per pass, never which distribution they come from. Lab 17 has the proof, the expected-length formula plotted above as "theory", and the 5090 recipe with a real draft model.

The tiny model here has 2 layers, so a verify pass is cheap and the wall-clock gain is small; the accept rate and tokens-per-pass numbers are the same quantities that decide the speedup on a 70B model.

Station 9

Mixture of experts: a router and four small MLPs

An MoE layer replaces one big MLP with several small ones and a router that sends each token to its top expert. Total parameters grow with the number of experts; the compute per token does not. The catch is balance: without a penalty the router happily sends everything to one expert. This station trains a top-1 MoE head on top of the pretrained model's hidden states, on the stories plus arithmetic mixture, and shows which expert each domain uses.

step 0loss –balance loss –max expert share –

Rows are domains (stories, arithmetic); columns are experts; the bar is the fraction of that domain's tokens routed to that expert. With the balance coefficient at 0 you will usually see one column take everything (routing collapse). With 0.01 the columns even out and, often, the two domains prefer different experts.

The balance loss

L_bal = E · Σ_e f_e · P_e, f_e = fraction of tokens routed to e, P_e = mean router probability for e

f is not differentiable (it comes from an argmax), P is. Their product is minimised when both are uniform, and the gradient flows through P, nudging the router away from over-used experts. That is the Switch Transformer loss; Lab 18 covers the z-loss, capacity factors, loss-free bias balancing, shared experts, and the 5090 recipe that trains a real MoE.

Active parameters here: one expert MLP per token. Total: all E of them. That ratio is the whole point of MoE at scale.
